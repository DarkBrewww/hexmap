How the right side was made

Schematic outline. The Census outline (133 points) is simplified with Douglas-Peucker, each edge is replaced by at most two segments on allowed directions, and slivers shorter than 2 squashed units are folded away: 24 segments. This step is ours; the paper starts from outlines that are already schematic.

Directions. Their code knows only rectilinear and octilinear directions. Before the run, x is squashed by tan 30°, which turns the hexagon's slanted sides into 45° sides; after the run it is stretched back. So the allowed directions here are 0°, 30°, 90° and 150°: the hexagon's three edge directions plus horizontal. "On allowed directions" below each side measures the share of the outline's length that runs in one of them (within 1°).

Hexagon inside, then a slide. Their method morphs between outlines that are nested or overlap, while this district's hexagon sits about 3.3 hex steps away on the grid. The hexagon is therefore placed inside the district where it has the most room, their code morphs onto it, and the slide to the grid position is added on top at constant speed. Their code also needs a cut that joins the two outlines; it runs horizontally from the hexagon's upper-left corner to the district's west edge, as the paper asks for a schematic cut line. Untick "Slide to grid position" to see their morph alone.

Run. Their MorphApp from tag "publication", run with -S OCTILINEAR -T GEODESIC on Java 11. The algorithm code is unmodified; the GeoTools classes it imports are replaced by 8 small stand-ins (a default JTS geometry factory, a WKT reader, and a shapefile export that is never called). Their correspondence: 3 matches, 3 insertions, 22 deletions, timed in 6 transition cells. Their own checks: self-crossings no, self-contained yes. The page plays their 7 keyframes with straight-line motion in between, exactly as their SVG output does. Checked over 2,001 in-between frames: every edge within 0.05° of an allowed direction, no crossings.
